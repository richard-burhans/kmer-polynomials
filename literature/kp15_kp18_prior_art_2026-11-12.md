## KP-15. Mismatches as syndromes

**Partly published.** The decoding is Clifford, Kociumaka & Porat 2019 (`clifford_2019_streaming-k-mismatch`,
SODA 2019). Their k-mismatch sketch (Definition 2.2, arXiv 1708.05223 line 216) is
sk_k(S) = (φ₀ … φ_{2k}, φ′₀ … φ′_k, ψ_r) over F_p with φ_j(S) = Σ S[i]·i^j. The difference of two sketches is a power-sum
system in the mismatch positions, which they solve with "the standard Peterson–Gorenstein–Zierler procedure" for
Reed–Solomon codes (lines 756-757). It decides HD ≤ k and returns every mismatch (position and both symbols) in
O(k log³ n) time from 3k + 3 field elements (Lemma 2.3, line 230; line 225). It rolls, and it composes under
concatenation (Lemma 2.6). The characteristic-2 predecessor is Clifford, Efremenko, Porat & Rothschild 2009, "From
coding theory to efficient pattern matching" (SODA, DOI 10.1137/1.9781611973068.85). Porat & Lipsky 2007 ("Improved
sketching of Hamming distance with error correcting", CPM, DOI 10.1007/978-3-540-73437-6_19) introduced sketches that
also locate the differences. Dodis et al. 2008 (`dodis_2008_fuzzy-extractors`, already held) names the syndrome of a
linear code as the secure sketch for Hamming distance.

How close to ours: KP-15 evaluates the k-mer polynomial at x, x², …, x^{2t}, so its locators are x^i. Clifford et
al.'s locators are the integer positions i (moments). Both are Reed–Solomon syndromes and decode the same way. The
difference is the parametrisation, not the idea.

What remains ours:
- the genomic application (no paper found applies these sketches to k-mers or reads), and the interaction with
  strand symmetry (syndromes of F and of R);
- reuse of the KP-6 hash: S₁ = F(x) is already the KP-6 hash, so t = 1 costs one extra evaluation, F(x²).

What does NOT follow, and should be dropped from the idea: **a mismatch-tolerant index.** Syndromes are compared
pairwise. To find which stored k-mer is near a query, you still need a candidate. The cheap route to candidates does not
need syndromes: any hash linear in the coefficients (KP-6, and also ntHash over GF(2)) gives each of the 3k Hamming-1
neighbours' hashes in O(1) as h(s) + δ·xⁱ, so neighbour enumeration against an exact-match table already works.
Locality-sensitive hashing for ungapped similarity (Buhler 2001, DOI 10.1093/bioinformatics/17.5.419) is the
established genomic route.

## KP-16. Reversal as x → 1/x

**The algebra is published; the hash adds nothing to KP-7.** Reversal as the reciprocal polynomial is textbook coding
theory: a cyclic code is reversible exactly when its generator is self-reciprocal (Massey 1964, "Reversible codes",
DOI 10.1016/S0019-9958(64)90438-3; want-listed, not read). DNA coding theory builds reverse and reverse-complement
constraints into cyclic codes over GF(4) and its rings this way (Gaborit & King 2005, DOI 10.1016/j.tcs.2004.11.004;
Abualrub, Ghrayeb & Zeng 2006, DOI 10.1016/j.jfranklin.2006.02.009; surveyed in `limbachiya_2016_dna-coding-theory`,
e.g. "self-reciprocal complement cyclic codes", survey line 711).

The proposed hash reduces to KP-7 exactly. From section 0, R(x) = −x^{k−1}·F(1/x), so F(1/x) = −x^{−(k−1)}·R(x): the
pair {F(x), F(1/x)} holds the same information as KP-7's {F(x), R(x)} at the same point, at the same cost. With
x = y², u = y^{−(k−1)}F(x) and v = y^{k−1}F(1/x), reverse complement sends (u, v) to (−v, −u), and

- u − v = y^{−(k−1)}·(F + R)(x), KP-7's degenerate e₁;
- u·v = −y^{−2(k−1)}·F·R, KP-7's degenerate e₂;
- the exact invariant is (u + λ)(λ − v), KP-7's (F + λ)(R + λ) rescaled.

Checked exhaustively at k = 4, 5, 6 over F_{q²}, q = 2⁶¹ − 1 (`kp16_moebius_check.py` in this directory): the identity
holds for every k-mer; u − v takes 81, 81 and 729 values (9^⌊k/2⌋) and u·v 70, 272 and 1,032, against 136, 512 and 2,080
strand classes; (u + λ)(λ − v) separates all classes. So there is no second proof route and no cheaper hash.

Searches for a hash built on evaluation at {x, 1/x} found none. That absence does not matter now.

## KP-17. The Klein four-group on {A, C, G, T}

**Published.** `zhang_2014_z-curve-review` (Current Genomics 15:78) states that I, R_x, R_y, R_z form "an invariant
subgroup … isomorphic to the Klein-4 group, or K4 group" (conversion line 78). R_x is A↔G, C↔T (transition), R_y is A↔C,
G↔T and R_z is A↔T, G↔C, "termed as the complementary transform" (lines 101-103). The Z-curve components
x = (A+G)−(C+T), y = (A+C)−(G+T), z = (A+T)−(G+C) (eq. 31b, line 308; first derived in Zhang & Zhang 1994, DOI
10.1080/07391102.1994.10508031) are the RY, MK and SW codings (line 316). They are the three nontrivial ±1 characters of
the group, the Hadamard rows (our gloss), and the complement R_z fixes z only (line 76). "The component z is related to
G+C content" (line 453): that is KP-17's complement-invariant SW channel. Earlier, `jimenez-montano_1996_hypercube-genetic-code`
gives the RY and SW bits, says the third partition "is not independent from the" former ones (lines 146-147), and names the
base exchanges "a Klein–4 group" (lines 150-151). Its β is the complement, γ the transition and α the two-bit transversion
(lines 1157-1158, 1210-1211). The group goes back to Bertman & Jungck 1979 (DOI 10.1093/oxfordjournals.jhered.a109281).
Fimmel, Danielli & Strüngmann 2013 (DOI 10.1016/j.jtbi.2013.07.027) and Gumbel et al. 2015 (DOI
10.1016/j.biosystems.2014.12.001) study the three dichotomic classes.

"Which encodings separate transitions from transversions" is also old in phylogenetics. Kimura's three-substitution-type
model (Kimura 1981, DOI 10.1073/pnas.78.1.454) is the group-based model on this Klein group. Its Fourier/Hadamard
analysis is Evans & Speed 1993 (DOI 10.1214/aos/1176349030) and Hendy, Penny & Steel 1994 (DOI 10.1073/pnas.91.8.3339).
All three are want-listed, not read.

What remains ours: hashing the SW projection as a cheap strand-symmetric channel. It needs only reversal symmetry, but it
keeps only the weak/strong pattern, 2^k of 4^k strings, so it is a filter, not a k-mer hash. Not found in the searches.

## KP-18. Burnside counting of canonical k-mers

**Published.** `wittler_2023_canonical-kmer-encoding`, Section 4: for an even-size alphabet with fixed-point-free
complement, odd k has no reverse-complement palindromes and |C| = σ^k/2, and even k gives ½(σ^k + σ^{k/2}) (conversion
lines 741-754). For DNA that is 4^k/2 and (4^k + 4^{k/2})/2, the IDEAS formulas. Wittler counts by pairing, not by
Burnside's lemma. For a group of order two the two are the same argument, and Burnside's lemma itself is textbook. Cite
Wittler. The exhaustive class counts above (136, 512, 2,080 at k = 4, 5, 6) agree.

## Worth prototyping?

- **KP-15, narrowly:** a two-k-mer "where do these differ" report from 2t extra evaluations is cheap to try, and as far
  as these searches reach no genomic tool uses it. It would be credited to Clifford et al. 2019, not claimed. The index
  version is not worth building: the neighbour lookup it wants is linear-hash enumeration, which needs no syndromes.
- KP-16, KP-17 and KP-18: no. KP-16 is KP-7; KP-17 and KP-18 are citations for the paper's encoding and sizing text.
