## KP-15. Mismatches as syndromes

**Partly published.** The decoding is Clifford, Kociumaka & Porat 2019 (`clifford_2019_streaming-k-mismatch`,
SODA 2019). Their k-mismatch sketch (Definition 2.2, arXiv 1708.05223 line 216) is
sk_k(S) = (φ₀ … φ_{2k}, φ′₀ … φ′_k, ψ_r) over F_p with φ_j(S) = Σ S[i]·i^j. The difference of two sketches is a power-sum
system in the mismatch positions, which they solve with "the standard Peterson–Gorenstein–Zierler procedure" for
Reed–Solomon codes (lines 756-757). It decides HD ≤ k and returns every mismatch (position and both symbols) in
O(k log³ n) time from 3k + 3 field elements (Lemma 2.3, line 230; line 225). It rolls, and it composes under
concatenation (Lemma 2.6). Dodis et al. 2008 (`dodis_2008_fuzzy-extractors`, already held) names the syndrome of a
linear code as the secure sketch for Hamming distance.

The decoding is older than the 2019 sketch. Clifford, Efremenko, Porat & Rothschild 2009
(`clifford_2009_coding-theory-pattern-matching`, SODA, DOI 10.1137/1.9781611973068.85) computes, for every alignment of a
pattern against a text, the 2k + 1 sums s_ℓ = Σ (p_j − t_{i+j})·(i + j)^ℓ, ℓ = 0 … 2k (equation 4.1), by FFT
cross-correlation in GF(2^⌈log 4m⌉), and decodes them with Berlekamp–Massey: P(z) = Π(z·x_i − 1), whose inverted roots
are the mismatch positions (Theorem 4.2). It names the BCH "key equation" (conversion line 161). Positions enter only as
field elements under "a mapping from [2m] to" the field (lines 306-309) that the paper requires but never specifies
(it calls only the alphabet map one to one).

The single-difference report is older still. Porat & Lipsky 2007 (`porat_2007_hamming-sketch-error-correcting`, CPM 2007,
LNCS 4580:173-182, DOI 10.1007/978-3-540-73437-6_19), Section 5: each position is hashed to buckets, and each bucket keeps
a_j = Σ r·i·x_i, b_j = Σ r·x_i, c_j = Σ r²·x_i, d_j = Σ r·x_i² (lines 400-403), r a random per-position weight. A bucket
with one mismatch gives its position as i′ = Δa_j/Δb_j, the weight as r′ = Δc_j/Δb_j, a check h′(i′, j) = r′ that the
bucket really held one mismatch (false pass below 1/p, Lemma 2), and both symbols from Δb_j and Δd_j (procedure on page
180). Layered sketches for k, k/2, … recover all mismatches in O(k log n) bits.

How close to ours: KP-15's single-substitution report is S₂/S₁ = xⁱ from F(x) and F(x²). Porat & Lipsky's is
Δa/Δb = i: the same move, a position-weighted linear sum divided by a plain one, with i in place of xⁱ. For t > 1,
KP-15 is Clifford et al. 2009's system with position i labelled xⁱ, a labelling that paper leaves open. With that
labelling s_ℓ is the difference polynomial evaluated at x^ℓ, which is exactly KP-15. Clifford et al. 2019 use the
integer i. Nothing in the parametrisation is new.

One thing the published versions have that the bare KP-15 report lacks is an explicit CHECK. From S₁ and S₂ alone,
two substitutions also give a ratio. Porat & Lipsky spend a third sum (c_j) to reject that case; Clifford et al. 2009
verify with an integer sum of squared differences; Clifford et al. 2019 carry a Karp–Rabin fingerprint. A KP-15
prototype needs one. Testing that S₂/S₁ is one of the k locators x⁰ … x^{k−1} is itself a check: for a random x in a
field of size q, two substitutions land on a locator with probability of order k²/q (our estimate, not measured), which
is negligible at q ≈ 2^122 but is not zero. S₃ = S₂²/S₁ (one more evaluation) tightens it further.

What remains ours:
- the genomic application (no paper found applies these sketches to k-mers or reads), and the interaction with
  strand symmetry (syndromes of F and of R). Clifford et al. 2009 mention genetics only as motivation (line 51);
  Porat & Lipsky name synchronisation;
- that the first syndrome is the KP-6 hash already computed, so the report costs F(x²) (and F(x³) for the check). This
  is an engineering convenience of our encoding, not a new idea: the published constructions all add sums to a sketch
  they already keep.

What does NOT follow, and should be dropped from the idea: **a mismatch-tolerant index.** Syndromes are compared
pairwise. To find which stored k-mer is near a query, you still need a candidate. The cheap route to candidates does not
need syndromes: any hash linear in the coefficients (KP-6, and also ntHash over GF(2)) gives each of the 3k Hamming-1
neighbours' hashes in O(1) as h(s) + δ·xⁱ, so neighbour enumeration against an exact-match table already works.
Locality-sensitive hashing for ungapped similarity (`buhler_2001_lsh-sequence-comparison`, DOI
10.1093/bioinformatics/17.5.419) is the established genomic route: it samples k random positions of long ungapped
seeds as the hash, and then counts mismatches pair by pair. It locates nothing algebraically.

## KP-16. Reversal as x → 1/x

**The algebra is published; the hash adds nothing to KP-7.** Reversal as the reciprocal polynomial is Massey 1964
(`massey_1964_reversible-codes`, DOI 10.1016/S0019-9958(64)90438-3, now read): the reversed word is
f_R(X) = X^{n−1}·f(X^{−1}) (equation 1), and a cyclic code is reversible exactly when its generator is self-reciprocal
(Theorem 1), that is, when its root set is closed under β ↦ β^{−1} (Theorem 1A). There is no complement in it. DNA
coding theory adds the complement to cyclic codes over GF(4) and its rings through self-reciprocal and related
polynomials (Abualrub, Ghrayeb & Zeng 2006, DOI 10.1016/j.jfranklin.2006.02.009, still unread; surveyed in
`limbachiya_2016_dna-coding-theory`, e.g. "self-reciprocal complement cyclic codes", survey line 711).

**Correction (2026-11-12, after reading it):** Gaborit & King 2005 (`gaborit_2005_linear-dna-codes`, DOI
10.1016/j.tcs.2004.11.004) was listed here as building the reverse constraint "this way". It does not. It treats
reversal as a permutation of coordinates in a code's automorphism group and the complement as translation by the
all-one word (x̂ = x + 1 over GF(4)), and credits the cyclic route to Rykov, Macula, Torney & White 2001 (ISIT, DOI
10.1109/isit.2001.936111, now want-listed). The word "reciprocal" does not occur in it.

The proposed hash reduces to KP-7 exactly. From section 0, R(x) = −x^{k−1}·F(1/x), so F(1/x) = −x^{−(k−1)}·R(x): the
pair {F(x), F(1/x)} holds the same information as KP-7's {F(x), R(x)} at the same point, at the same cost. With
x = y², u = y^{−(k−1)}F(x) and v = y^{k−1}F(1/x), reverse complement sends (u, v) to (−v, −u), and

- u − v = y^{−(k−1)}·(F + R)(x), KP-7's degenerate e₁;
- u·v = −y^{−2(k−1)}·F·R, KP-7's degenerate e₂;
- the exact invariant is (u + λ)(λ − v), KP-7's (F + λ)(R + λ) rescaled.

Checked exhaustively at k = 4, 5, 6 over F_{q²}, q = 2⁶¹ − 1 (`kp16_moebius_check.py` in this directory): the identity
holds for every k-mer; u − v takes 81, 81 and 729 values (9^⌊k/2⌋) and u·v 70, 272 and 1,032, against 136, 512 and 2,080
strand classes; (u + λ)(λ − v) separates all classes. So there is no second proof route and no cheaper hash.

Searches for a hash built on evaluation at {x, 1/x} found none. That absence does not matter now.

## KP-17. The Klein four-group on {A, C, G, T}

**Published.** `zhang_2014_z-curve-review` (Current Genomics 15:78) states that I, R_x, R_y, R_z form "an invariant
subgroup … isomorphic to the Klein-4 group, or K4 group" (conversion line 78). R_x is A↔G, C↔T (transition), R_y is A↔C,
G↔T and R_z is A↔T, G↔C, "termed as the complementary transform" (lines 101-103). The Z-curve components
x = (A+G)−(C+T), y = (A+C)−(G+T), z = (A+T)−(G+C) (eq. 31b, line 308; first derived in Zhang & Zhang 1994, DOI
10.1080/07391102.1994.10508031) are the RY, MK and SW codings (line 316). They are the three nontrivial ±1 characters of
the group, the Hadamard rows (our gloss), and the complement R_z fixes z only (line 76). "The component z is related to
G+C content" (line 453): that is KP-17's complement-invariant SW channel. Earlier, `jimenez-montano_1996_hypercube-genetic-code`
gives the RY and SW bits, says the third partition "is not independent from the" former ones (lines 146-147), and names the
base exchanges "a Klein–4 group" (lines 150-151). Its β is the complement, γ the transition and α the two-bit transversion
(lines 1157-1158, 1210-1211). The group is older: `bertman_1979_group-graph-genetic-code` (DOI
10.1093/oxfordjournals.jhered.a109281) uses exactly these three exchanges (α: A↔C, U↔G; β: A↔U, C↔G; γ: A↔G, C↔U,
Figure 4) as a Klein group on codon doublets, and credits them to Danckwerts & Neubert 1975, "Symmetries of genetic
code-doublets", J. Mol. Evol. 5:327-332 (DOI 10.1007/bf01732219, now want-listed). `fimmel_2013_dichotomic-classes`
(DOI 10.1016/j.jtbi.2013.07.027) shows the three swaps are the only fixed-point-free involutions of the bases, which
with the identity form the Klein V group, and pairs them with the SW, MK and RY splits; its dichotomic classes are
non-linear functions of two bases, not characters. Gumbel et al. 2015 (DOI 10.1016/j.biosystems.2014.12.001) is not in
the library.

"Which encodings separate transitions from transversions" is also old in phylogenetics. Kimura's three-substitution-type
model (Kimura 1981, DOI 10.1073/pnas.78.1.454, not in the library) is the group-based model on this Klein group, and
its Fourier/Hadamard analysis is now read. `evans_1993_phylogenetic-invariants` (DOI 10.1214/aos/1176349030) identifies
{A, G, C, T} with Z₂ ⊕ Z₂ (A → (0,0), G → (0,1), C → (1,0), T → (1,1)), shows each Kimura 3ST generator is a random walk
on the group, and analyses it with the group's characters (table on page 360). `hendy_1994_discrete-fourier-trees` (DOI
10.1073/pnas.91.8.3339) codes A (0,0), G (1,0), C (0,1), T (1,1), takes a substitution's type as the difference mod 2
(type I transitions, II A↔C/G↔T, III A↔T/C↔G), and inverts the Hadamard transform to count each type. Under either
labelling the three non-trivial ±1 characters are exactly the MK, RY and SW indicators; neither paper names them so
(our computation from their tables). So "which encodings separate transitions from transversions" has a standard answer
from 1993-1994: the characters of this group.

What remains ours: hashing the SW projection as a cheap strand-symmetric channel. It needs only reversal symmetry, but it
keeps only the weak/strong pattern, 2^k of 4^k strings, so it is a filter, not a k-mer hash. Not found in the searches.

## KP-18. Burnside counting of canonical k-mers

**Published.** `wittler_2023_canonical-kmer-encoding`, Section 4: for an even-size alphabet with fixed-point-free
complement, odd k has no reverse-complement palindromes and |C| = σ^k/2, and even k gives ½(σ^k + σ^{k/2}) (conversion
lines 741-754). For DNA that is 4^k/2 and (4^k + 4^{k/2})/2, the IDEAS formulas. Wittler counts by pairing, not by
Burnside's lemma. For a group of order two the two are the same argument, and Burnside's lemma itself is textbook. Cite
Wittler. The exhaustive class counts above (136, 512, 2,080 at k = 4, 5, 6) agree.

## Worth prototyping?

- **KP-15, narrowly:** a two-k-mer "where do these differ" report from extra evaluations is cheap to try, and as far
  as these searches reach no genomic tool uses it. It is a published technique applied to k-mers: credit Porat & Lipsky
  2007 for the single-difference ratio and its check, Clifford et al. 2009 for the general decoding, and Clifford et al.
  2019 for the rolling sketch. Include a single-substitution check (the locator-membership test of S₂/S₁, and
  optionally S₃ = S₂²/S₁); without one, a pair differing at two positions can be reported as one. The index
  version is not worth building: the neighbour lookup it wants is linear-hash enumeration, which needs no syndromes.
- KP-16, KP-17 and KP-18: no. KP-16 is KP-7; KP-17 and KP-18 are citations for the paper's encoding and sizing text.
